# Story Bible

## Who this is for
Young adults (18–30). Respect their intelligence: no talking down, no cheerleading, no
lectures. They will notice if the story is rigged in either direction, so keep it honest.

## Voice
- **Third person, present tense.** "Mateo walks into the cafeteria." Use template tokens
  for the name and pronouns (`{name}`, `{subj}`, `{poss}`, …; see `content-schema.md`).
  Never "you" in narrative text.
- **Close third.** Stay near the child's point of view and feelings, at an age-appropriate
  level. A four-year-old notices the cubby with their name on it, not the tuition bill;
  the narrator can mention the bill.
- **Warm and observant.** Specific, a little funny, honest about hard things.
- **Concrete over abstract.** Prices, bus routes, waitlists, shift schedules, the group
  chat, the smell of the gym. Brand-free.

## Showing inequity
The story's job is to make inequity visible. Do it like this:

1. **Show the constraint concretely, in the scene.** "The Montessori school down the road
   costs $14,000 a year. Last year {poss} mom made $31,000." Not: "Private preschool is
   expensive for low-income families." (Numbers come from `content/data/`, never typed in.)
2. **Let research notes carry the evidence.** Scenes show; the end-of-stage research note
   gives the statistic and the source.
3. **Name systems, not villains.** Admissions offices, landlords, and bosses follow rules
   and incentives. Point at the rule (legacy preferences, property-tax school funding,
   unpaid internships), not at a cartoon bad guy.
4. **Never blame the child or the family.** A family that can't afford preschool didn't
   make a mistake. A teenager who works 20 hours a week isn't "less committed" than one who
   does debate.
5. **Advantage is shown too.** Show what money and connections buy (a tutor, a summer
   internship through a parent's friend, a parent who knows how to appeal a grade) so
   readers from any background can see the whole system.
6. **Agency is real.** Choices matter and the story honors effort. The point is that the
   same effort pays off differently depending on where you start.

## Fairness rules
1. **Choices change odds, not fates.** A child whose parents didn't finish high school
   can become a surgeon; a child with every advantage can struggle.
2. **Every choice has a cost and a benefit.**
3. **No moralizing about personal choices.** Partying, quitting, skipping college, or
   choosing a trade is never framed as a moral failure; ambition is never framed as shallow.
4. **No stereotypes.** Don't tie behaviors, values, or intelligence to class, race,
   religion, region, gender, or family structure. Don't infer anything from the child's name.
5. **Hard topics with care.** Illness, job loss, divorce, money trouble, and grief may
   appear. Treat them briefly and with dignity. No graphic content. Alcohol and parties
   may appear; drugs only in passing; no self-harm content.

## Policy framing
The ending connects the child's life to policy. Rules:
- **Evidence-based.** Each policy cites research on what it does, from `sources.md`.
- **Nonpartisan.** Never name parties, politicians, or campaigns. Describe the policy, the
  evidence, and the trade-offs (cost, who pays, open questions).
- **Specific and tied to the story.** "Need-based aid that covered {name}'s full net price"
  connects to R6; "universal pre-K" connects to D3. No generic slogans.
- **Empowering.** Point to ways to learn more and act (find your elected officials, local
  school board meetings, nonpartisan research organizations), with links. Don't tell
  readers what to think; give them what they need to decide.

## Length and rhythm
- Decision scenes: 80–150 words, 2–3 short paragraphs, ending on the tension of the choice.
- Event cards: 60–120 words, plus 1–2 sentences of impact.
- Result cards: 40–100 words of text around the data table.
- Research notes: under 90 words, one key statistic, source linked.
- Choice labels: under 70 characters, third person actions ("{Subj} joins the robotics
  club"), not outcomes ("{Subj} becomes a great engineer").
- Outcome lines: one or two sentences showing a gain and a cost.

## The child and family
- The child's name and pronouns come from the random draw. Gender doesn't change the story
  or outcomes unless the author decides otherwise (see `brief.md`).
- Race and ethnicity aren't stated (see `brief.md`). Don't hint at them through names of
  family members, foods, or places.
- Parents and family members get specific, human details (a dad who sings in the car, a
  grandmother who keeps score at every game).
- **Family structure varies** by playthrough: two-parent, single-parent, grandparent-led,
  blended, and same-sex-parent households, assigned at random and weighted by national
  shares (`census_acs`). It sets flavor text and, where research supports it, household
  income.

## Words to avoid
- In labels and outcomes: "good," "bad," "right," "wrong," "smart," "stupid," "lazy,"
  "failure," "winner," "loser."
- "Poor kids," "rich kids," "underprivileged," "at-risk." Prefer specifics: "families
  earning under $40,000," "a school where most students qualify for free lunch."
- Jargon without explanation (FAFSA, Pell, net price, legacy). Explain on first use.

## Stage moods
| Stage | Mood | Typical details |
|---|---|---|
| Beginnings | Tender, a bit wry | Hospital, first apartment or house, family stories |
| Early childhood | Small and sensory | Cereal, cartoons, a daycare cubby, a grandparent's kitchen |
| Elementary | Curious, rule-learning | Spelling tests, recess politics, a first team jersey |
| Middle school | Awkward, intense | Lockers, group chats, who sits where |
| High school | High stakes, high energy | Parking lot, test scores, first job, first heartbreak |
| After high school | Disorienting, freeing | Dorm or apartment, financial aid portal, first paycheck |
| Early adulthood | Reflective, uncertain | Rent, loan statements, friends' weddings, calls home |
